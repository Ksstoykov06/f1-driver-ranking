F1 Driver Model: 2018–2026 Pilot

Two separate models, one using every lap and one using only official results, both trying to work out how good each driver is once the car is taken away.

Where it stands

Both models put Verstappen first, and by a clear margin. In identical cars, he averages about P in the lap model and P in the results model. The next driver is more than two places behind in both. Below him the models share 8 of their top 10, but the order is close enough that most neighbouring places can't be separated.

The method works well enough to keep developing. Both models predict teammate battles better than simple benchmarks and give stable answers. Adaptability, wet-weather skill and age are not yet working properly, and those are the parts you most wanted, so they're the first things to decide on.

This ranks only what's visible since 2018, using 192 races. It says nothing about Senna, Schumacher or Alonso at his peak, and a driver's score covers only the seasons in this window. Treat it as a test of the method, not a verdict on the all-time question.

What each model does

Both models answer the same question and share the same core idea: performance is driver skill plus car strength, adjusted for wet conditions, age and how that particular season went. They differ in the evidence they use.

MODEL A · LAP DATA

How fast was each lap?

  1. Keep only representative laps: push laps in qualifying, clean-air green-flag laps in the race.
  2. Remove fuel, tyres and track conditions, leaving one pace gap per driver per session.
  3. Split that gap into driver, car and season effects. Results are in percent of lap time.
  4. Measure racecraft: places gained or lost beyond what pace and grid predict.
MODEL B · RESULTS ONLY

Who finished ahead of whom?

  1. Take the qualifying order and finishing order from each weekend.
  2. Estimate who tends to beat whom, using rank-ordered logit (Plackett-Luce).
  3. Split that into driver, car and season effects, accounting for grid position and how hard each track is to pass on.
  4. This is the only version that can reach back to 1980, so comparing it with Model A shows what we lose without lap data.

Both then share the last step. Mechanical retirements are left out, while solo crashes count against the driver. Each driver then goes through simulated weekends in identical cars, with some rain and a random car fit each time. Their score is their average finishing position in a 20-car field, which lets us compare models that work in different units.

Step 1: Choosing which laps count

This is your point about one hero lap flattering a driver. Model A averages all of a driver's push laps in the dry instead of taking only the fastest. Out-laps, in-laps, laps under yellow flags and deleted laps are dropped. Of about qualifying laps recorded, count. In races, of do.

In a wet session this doesn't work, because a drying track makes later laps faster for everyone. So wet qualifying uses each driver's best lap in each segment instead.

Step 2: Separating the driver from the car

Teammates share a car, so the gap between them says something about the drivers. Those comparisons then link up: Gasly raced alongside both Verstappen and Tsunoda, so those three are linked, and links like that form a chain across the grid. Everything a team's drivers have in common becomes the car's strength for that season.

Where it isn't working yet

Adaptability barely varies
How much each driver's level moves from season to season, Model A, with 90% ranges. A lower figure means steadier.
Wet race skill is very uncertain
Adjustment in wet races, top 10 of Model B. Right of zero means better in the wet. Most ranges cross zero.

Adaptability: The ranges are far wider than the differences between drivers, so the data mostly falls back on a shared average. Your best-fit and poor-fit rankings therefore come out almost identical to the main score. As it stands, adaptability doesn't really influence the ranking, which isn't what we agreed. It also can't yet tell a bad car fit from a poor season.

Wet weather: With only wet races and wet qualifying sessions, there's too little evidence. Model B makes Hamilton look poor in the wet, which goes against his reputation, while Model A sees almost no effect at all. The wet label also relies on tyre choice, so short showers are missed.

Age: Teammates age together, so the data can't separate a steady age trend from driver and season effects. A prior holds that trend near zero, which means the age curve is mostly an assumption. The curvature estimates also differ between models.

Known gaps: Team orders aren't modelled, so number-two drivers may be underrated. Bahrain 2018 has no lap data, and Spa 2021 is excluded from the race models. Sprints aren't used yet. Collisions don't count as anyone's fault in the main run. The wet labels come from lap data, so going back before 2018 will need a hand-built list.

What to decide next

1. Rebuild adaptability (my top recommendation)Stop trying to estimate it from season noise. Measure it directly: how a driver performs against a teammate in the first races after a team move or rule change, compared with later. This gives fewer data points but a clearer meaning, and we could then give it an explicit weight in the score.
2. Hand-label wet sessionsReplace the tyre rule with a checked list of races and qualifying sessions. It's needed for 1980 onwards anyway. You'd also want to decide whether Donington-style mixed races count as wet.
3. Add driver improvementLet each driver's level drift smoothly over a career. This would separate a young Verstappen from a peak one, and could replace the weak age curve.
4. Deal with team ordersDecide which seasons or races to flag, such as Bottas at Mercedes or Pérez at Red Bull. This is a judgement call and F1 knowledge matters here, so your input would help most.
5. Add sprints, then test Model B as a stand-in for Model ASprints add data on the current grid. We'd then measure how closely Model B can match Model A using results alone, which tells us how far to trust it before 2018.

I'd do 1 and 2 before extending backwards, since neither adaptability nor wet skill would improve just by adding older seasons. Also worth deciding: which single headline score you want, because main, peak and career value favour different drivers.
